# Habit Control & "Detox" — The Intervention Science

> The user asked to learn everything about controlling habits, "detox", and their
> solutions. Half of what the internet says about this is wrong, and the wrong
> half is the profitable half. This doc separates them, then turns the surviving
> science into design.
>
> Companions: `HABITS-ROUTINES-DEEP.md` (how habits form), `BEHAVIOR-SCIENCE-DEEP.md`
> (the clinical techniques), `PORN-MASTURBATION-CONTROL.md` (the compulsive case).

---

## 0. The two headlines

1. **"Dopamine detox" does not exist.** You cannot fast from a neurotransmitter.
   Every credible source says so. What people *actually* do when they "detox"
   is CBT — and that works for reasons that have nothing to do with dopamine.
2. **For most digital overuse, reduction beats abstinence.** The evidence is
   consistent enough to build on: cutting down holds better than quitting cold.

Arise must be the app that says this out loud. Selling "reset your dopamine in
7 days" would be the single most profitable lie in this category, and it is
forbidden by red line 6 (guilt/fear copy) and by our own honesty standard.

## 1. Dopamine detox — the honest verdict

### 1.1 What the claim is
"Dopamine detoxing" claims that heavy exposure to stimulating behaviour floods
the brain with dopamine, causing "tolerance", so you must abstain to "reset
your receptors" and feel normal pleasure again.

### 1.2 Why it is false
- **You cannot deplete dopamine by avoiding stimulation.** Dopamine is a
  naturally occurring neurotransmitter and hormone; it does not fall because you
  stopped scrolling. Harvard Health (2020): *"it doesn't actually decrease when
  you avoid overstimulating activities, so a dopamine 'fast' doesn't actually
  lower your dopamine."*
- **Dopamine is not "the pleasure chemical".** It encodes reward prediction
  error and *motivation/wanting* — incentive salience — not enjoyment itself.
  The "flood then tolerance" story misstates the mechanism (The Scientist, 2024).
- **What the method actually is:** psychiatrist Cameron Sepah's "Dopamine
  Fasting 2.0" is explicitly *not* about lowering dopamine — it is a
  **CBT-based protocol for reducing impulsive behaviours**. Fei (2022,
  *Lifestyle Medicine*) states the focus "should be on reducing the impulsive
  behaviors", and that the catchy name caused the misinterpretation.
- **It can harm.** Cureus (2024) literature review: extreme forms (strict
  isolation, severe restriction) are associated with loneliness, anxiety, and
  malnutrition. The Cleveland Clinic's verdict: *"There is no such thing as a
  true dopamine detox"* — the accurate term is CBT.
- **The timeline is wrong too.** People expect a week to "reset". Real change in
  what the brain finds rewarding takes **weeks to months** of sustained change,
  not a deprivation weekend.

### 1.3 What is true underneath — and what Arise ships instead
The experience people report after a "detox" is real; the mechanism they were
sold is not. The real mechanisms:

| Real mechanism | What it is | Arise's name for it |
|---|---|---|
| **Cue removal** | The trigger stops firing, so the urge stops being generated | *Quiet hours* |
| **Friction** | The behaviour takes more steps than the alternative | *Friction* |
| **Reward re-learning** | New behaviours get reinforced; the old one stops paying | *Progression* |
| **Replacement** | A competing response occupies the same slot | *Substitution* |
| **Rest** | Stress/sleep load falls, restoring goal-directed control | *Foundation* |

**Rule: Arise never uses the word "detox" in UI copy.** Say what is happening:
"cues are quiet", "the door is further away", "you are re-learning what pays".
Naming the mechanism is more credible and more durable than the fad.

## 2. Digital detox — what the evidence actually supports

### 2.1 The evidence is weaker than the marketing
- **Radtke et al. (2022)** systematic review: 21 studies, 12 RCTs, 2013–2020.
  No consistent definition of "digital detox"; no consistency in how
  effectiveness was measured; findings inconsistent.
- **2025 preregistered meta-analysis** (10 studies, 4,674 participants): **no
  significant effect** of social-media abstinence on positive psychological
  well-being. Substantial between-study variation.
- **2024 state-of-the-art review**: no consistent definition; reducing time is
  more consistently beneficial than promoting total abstinence.
- **Wilcockson et al. (2019, *Addictive Behaviors*)**: smartphone abstinence
  produced mood fluctuation, anxiety, and craving in heavy users — i.e. the
  withdrawal is real, which is why the abstinence framing feels true.

### 2.2 Reduction beats abstinence — the strongest single result
**Brailovskaia et al. (2023)**, *Finding the "sweet spot" of smartphone use*,
N = 619, three arms:

| Arm | n | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 7-day total abstinence | 200 | life satisfaction and activity rose |
| **Reduce by 1 hour/day** | **226** | **effects were stronger and remained more stable at 4 months** |
| Control | 193 | — |

Conclusion in their words: *"a complete smartphone abstinence is not necessary."*

A 72-hour instant-messaging abstinence study found craving and withdrawal during
the fast, stable positive affect, and only a **small-to-moderate** reduction in
use one week later (*d* ≈ 0.x) — i.e. the effect decays.

### 2.3 What this means for Arise
- **Default to reduction, offer abstinence.** Not dogma — a per-behaviour
  choice, with the evidence stated in the UI.
- **Honesty about effect size.** Modest, decaying effects. Do not promise
  transformation.
- **Withdrawal is real and short.** Warn the user so they don't read the
  irritability as failure.
- **Duration shorter than the habit.** A 7-day sprint is a *demo*, not a cure.
  The product is the months after it.

## 3. The self-control ladder — the backbone of this doc

**Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016), *Situational Strategies for Self-Control*,
Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(1):35–55**, built on **Gross (1998)**.
Impulses develop in stages; you can intervene at any stage; **the earlier the
intervention, the more effective it is.**

| # | Strategy family | What it looks like in real life | In a habit app | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Situation selection** | don't buy the snacks; don't install the app; sit away from the bar | choose your cues, device setup, quiet hours | **highest** |
| 2 | **Situation modification** | ask the waiter to take the bread away; phone in another room | environment changes, friction, one-time setup | **high** |
| 3 | **Attentional deployment** | look away; distract; focus on a different feature | attention redirect, a competing task | moderate |
| 4 | **Cognitive change** | reappraise: "this is a wave, not a need" | reframing cards, urge-surfing script | moderate |
| 5 | **Response modulation** | white-knuckle it; suppress the urge | "I resisted" logging | **lowest** |

### 3.1 The evidence is unusually clean
- Students **rated** early strategies as dramatically more effective than late
  ones (Duckworth et al. 2016, *A Stitch in Time*).
- **Randomised**: students assigned to implement **situation modification** were
  more likely to meet their academic goals the following week than controls —
  and the effect was mediated by *reduced feelings of temptation*.
- Trait self-control's link to well-being is **mediated** by these strategies —
  self-controlled people are not resisting more, they are **encountering less to
  resist**.

### 3.2 The self-critique Arise has to accept
**Arise v1 as currently specced sits at rung 5.** It rewards what you did after
the impulse fired: you log, you complete, you earn XP. That is the weakest rung,
and the one every habit app already occupies.

The fix is structural, not cosmetic:

- Every quest carries a **strategy tag** — which rung it acts on.
- Onboarding produces **at least one rung-1 and one rung-2 action** before it
  produces a rung-5 tracker. The first-run experience must ship a
  *situation-selection* act: remove one cue.
- The System's language names the rung: "You changed the room, not your will."
- **A quest that cannot name its rung is not a quest yet.**

This single change is the difference between a tracker and an intervention.

## 4. Craving — the mechanics, so the app can teach them

- **Cue-induced craving**: the cue elicits the urge before any decision. This is
  why "deciding to quit" fails — it happens after the cue.
- **The wave**: urges rise, peak, and fall. Peak typically within minutes, not
  hours. The practical rule people can use: **wait ~10 minutes and the peak
  passes.**
- **Urge surfing** (Marlatt): observe the urge without acting or suppressing;
  it extinguishes. See `BEHAVIOR-SCIENCE-DEEP.md` §C.
- **Extinction is context-specific**: the urge can extinguish at home and return
  on holiday. Hence the "context reset" flow (see `HABITS-ROUTINES-DEEP.md` §4).
- **Craving is not a command, and not evidence of need.** The most useful
  sentence the app can teach: *"This is a wave. You are not required to act."*

**Design:** a *Surf* control on every urge-bearing screen. One tap starts a
10-minute timer with a breathing pacer and a single line of copy. No XP for
surfing (that would pay for the wrong thing), but a quiet, private log entry.

## 5. Reduction or abstinence — decide per behaviour

| Signal | Lean reduction | Lean abstinence |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviour is overuse of something useful | ✔ | |
| Behaviour has no function for the user | | ✔ |
| Strong, discrete cue + binge pattern | | ✔ (for a defined period) |
| Compulsive with escalation and impairment | | ✔ initially, then review |
| Abstinence repeatedly triggers AVE and collapse | ✔ | |
| User has tried and rejected abstinence before | ✔ | |

**Rule:** the app offers both, states the evidence briefly, and **records the
choice as a strategy the user owns** — never as a medical instruction. Arise is
not a clinician.

## 6. Lapse, relapse, and the two failure modes

### 6.1 The Abstinence Violation Effect (Marlatt & Gordon)
After a lapse, people attribute it to **internal, stable, global** causes —
"I'm weak", "I always do this", "I'm a failure". That attribution, plus guilt
and shame, is what converts a lapse into a full relapse. Attribution to a
**specific high-risk situation** ("I was tired and it was in my hand") predicts
recovery instead.

### 6.2 The "what the hell" effect
One cookie → "I've ruined it" → the whole packet. The rule is broken, so the
reason to hold is gone. This is a *cognitive* failure, not a moral one.

### 6.3 Self-compassion is not complacency
- Self-compassion after a lapse predicts **less negative affect** and **greater
  perceived control in the hours after**, and is not associated with later
  overeating (dietary-lapse studies; Neff's model).
- Smoking-lapse research: **self-efficacy** predicts progression from one lapse
  to the next; a longer prior abstinence period actually *potentiated* the guilt
  effect — the more you had to lose, the harder the fall.
- Marlatt's own claim holds: **lapse → relapse is not inevitable.**

### 6.4 The post-lapse protocol (what Arise does)
1. **Acknowledge in <10 seconds, no penalty.** No streak reset animation, no
   red, no sound. Losing the streak is already enough.
2. **Attribute to the situation, never the person.** The app asks one question:
   "What was around you?" — never "why did you fail?"
3. **Log the cue**, not the shame. That entry becomes data for the ladder.
4. **Resume within 24 hours.** "Never miss twice" is the rule; the second miss
   is the actual relapse.
5. **No penalty XP.** Removing progress for a lapse is the mechanism that
   produces AVE. Red line.

## 7. The platform underneath: sleep and stress

Self-control is not a personality trait standing on nothing. It sits on biology,
and the biology is measurable.

### 7.1 Sleep — strong enough to build on
**Chen et al. (2017), *Journal of Neuroscience*, 37(49):11979** — one night of
sleep deprivation produced a **deficit in goal-directed control and overreliance
on habit**, with fMRI evidence; goal-directed performance after deprivation
correlated with **baseline working-memory capacity**.

Translation: **a tired person is not making choices at all — they are running
habits.** Pushing a tired user to "try harder" is not just unkind, it targets the
one system that is offline.

**Design rule (non-obvious, and no competitor does it):** on a low-sleep day the
System should **lower demand**, not raise it. Quests shrink; the daily target
drops; copy shifts from "push" to "hold the line". This is the opposite of every
streak app, and the science is on this side.

### 7.2 Stress — contested, and must be labelled so
The famous claim is that acute stress shifts control from goal-directed to
habitual (Schwabe & Wolf 2009, 2010).

**But the replications failed.** Smeets et al. (2023, *Neurobiology of Stress*),
two **preregistered exact replications**: no reliable shift; in their sample
even non-stressed participants failed to show goal-directed control, rendering
the test inapt. A 2025 *PLOS ONE* study likewise found **no evidence** for
increased habitual or decreased goal-directed control after acute stress.

**Verdict: treat "stress makes you habitual" as plausible but unproven.** Build
the *humane* response anyway (reduce load under stress), but do not state it as
fact in user-facing copy. This is exactly the kind of claim Arise must be able
to cite honestly — see §10.

### 7.3 Willpower beliefs — a design trap
**Job, Dweck & Walton (2010), *Psychological Science* 21(11):1686**: people who
believe willpower is **unlimited** do not show ego depletion; those who believe
it is a limited resource do. Study 2 *manipulated* the belief and replicated it.

**Consequence: never build a willpower meter.** A bar that empties teaches the
limited theory, then creates the depletion it predicts, then gives the user a
readymade excuse. Combined with the failed ego-depletion replications, the
"willpower as fuel" model is both unsupported and actively harmful.

## 8. The active-ingredient audit (BCTTv1)

**Michie et al. (2013)**: 93 behaviour-change techniques in 16 groups — the
closest thing the field has to a periodic table.

**Michie et al. (2009)** meta-regression, 122 evaluations, N = 44,747,
overall **d = 0.31**:
- **Self-monitoring explained the most between-study heterogeneity (13%)** — it
  is the strongest single ingredient.
- **Self-monitoring + at least one other control-theory technique: 0.42 vs 0.26**
  for everything else.

So the winning recipe is not a technique, it is a **combination anchored on
self-monitoring**.

### 8.1 Arise v1 audit
| BCT group | Technique | v1 status |
|---|---|---|
| Goals & planning | Goal setting (behaviour) | ✔ |
| Goals & planning | Action planning (when/where) | ✔ (cue field) |
| Goals & planning | **Coping planning** | ✖ **missing** |
| Feedback & monitoring | Self-monitoring of behaviour | ✔ (the core loop) |
| Feedback & monitoring | Feedback on behaviour | ✔ (XP/status) |
| Antecedents | Restructuring the physical environment | ✖ missing |
| Antecedents | Adding objects to the environment | ✖ missing |
| Antecedents | **Reduce prompts/cues** | ✖ missing |
| Repetition | Habit formation | ✔ (thesis) |
| Reward | Self-reward | ✔ |
| Comparison | **Demonstration of the behaviour** | ✖ missing |
| Assist | **Instruction on how to perform** | ✖ missing |
| Identity | **Identification of self as role model** | ✖ missing |

**Three additions that cost almost nothing and close the biggest gaps:**
**coping planning** (what you will do when the cue fires), **reduce prompts/cues**
(quiet hours), and **instruction on how to perform** (one line per quest, not a
lecture). All three are rung-1/rung-2 work — see §3.

## 9. The environment is adversarial — say so

- **Lyngs et al. (CHI 2019)** reviewed **367** digital self-control tools:
  **74%** do blocking/removal; **23%** manipulate the *delay* component of the
  expected value of control. The market has correctly converged on early-rung
  intervention.
- **The circumvention paradox** (see `APP-LANDSCAPE.md`): a blocked user learns
  to route around the block, and the tool loses.
- **Supernormal stimuli**: modern products are hyper-optimised for capture. A
  user failing to resist is often a *normal brain in a rigged environment*, not
  a weak person. Naming this is both true and relieving — and it is the
  anti-shame position.

**Design consequence:** Arise is the one app on the phone that does **not**
compete for attention. Sessions end. There is no feed, no infinite scroll, no
"come back" nudge. Red line 6 already forbids engagement-driven notifications;
this is the positive form of the same rule: **the app should be boring to
overuse.**

## 11. Problem → solution → feature

| Problem | Evidence-based solution | Arise feature |
|---|---|---|
| "I keep doing it without deciding" | cue removal, situation selection | quiet hours; cue audit at onboarding |
| "I can't stop once I start" | friction, delay | friction field; 10-second delay |
| "The urge is unbearable" | urge surfing | Surf timer + breathing pacer |
| "I quit for a week then collapsed" | reduction not abstinence; no AVE | taper plans; no-penalty lapse log |
| "One slip ruins everything" | attribution retraining | post-lapse protocol (§6.4) |
| "I'm exhausted and it all falls apart" | lower demand when sleep is low | adaptive daily target |
| "I don't know what to actually do" | instruction + coping planning | one-line how-to; "when X, I will Y" |
| Nothing sticks past week 3 | self-monitoring + control theory | the core loop, with a strategy tag |
